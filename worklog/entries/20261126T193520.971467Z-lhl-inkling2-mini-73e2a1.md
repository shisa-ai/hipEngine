# Eight waves share one staged activation tile in the expert kernel

## Summary

The grouped WMMA projection of the prompt block's experts was the largest part of a prefill: 792 of
1,690 ms at 2,057 prompt tokens and 248 of 455 ms at 575. It is now 458 of 1,304 ms and 151 of
343 ms, and the first token arrives 16% sooner at 2,057 prompt tokens and 17% sooner at 575. The
kernel's arithmetic did not change: its output is bit for bit that of the previous kernel.

### Where the time was

A synthetic-routing microbenchmark (1,024 rows, 128 experts, top 8, skewed popularity) and variants
of the kernel that drop one thing each, timed with device events:

| Variant | 1,024 rows | 575 rows |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| The committed kernel | 4.40 ms | 4.01 ms |
| Without the matrix instructions | 0.29 ms | 0.20 ms |
| Without the unpacking | 3.93 ms | 3.05 ms |
| With every lane reading one activation row | 2.32 ms | 2.12 ms |
| Without the matrix instructions or the unpacking, one activation row | 1.91 ms | 1.67 ms |

The kernel was bound by its activation reads. Each wave read its 64 rows in 32-byte pieces, once per
32-column tile, so the 72 column tiles of a projection fetched the same rows 72 times: 2.1 ms of the
4.40. The matrix instructions were 1.75 ms, and the unpacking 0.4.

A separate probe of the wave32 matrix instruction gives its latency, about 210 cycles, and its rate:
one wave issues one 16x16x16 product in about 60 cycles, and a SIMD needs four waves to reach its
full rate, 119 TFLOP/s over the GPU. The kernel's 152 vector registers left two waves to a SIMD. So
wave64 was not the answer, and neither was a cheaper unpacking.

### What changed

- A block is eight wave32 and 256 output columns. The 64 activation rows of a k-tile are fetched
  into shared memory once for the eight waves, the next k-tile fetched before the compute of the
  current one and stored after it. The kernel takes 176 registers and the launch bound is two
  blocks a compute unit, so a block's eight waves reside together (inferred from the bound and the
  register count; the occupancy was not measured).
- The nine words of an NVFP4 block (four scale bytes, 32 bytes of codes) are read one block ahead;
  the k-loop runs four k-tiles at a time so the block in registers is consumed while the next is
  fetched.
- The row fragments past an expert's rows in its last group are padding and take no product. At 575
  prompt tokens half of the padded rows were padding.
- One activation fragment is live at a time; the dequantized weights of both column fragments stay.
- Waves past the output columns of the last block fetch and reach the barrier and compute nothing,
  so every output count that is a multiple of 32 still runs. An input width that is not a multiple
  of 64 values ends in direct k-tiles, so Q8_0 keeps widths of 32 mod 64.

### Variants that did not make it

- Four waves and 128 columns, no prefetch: first token 0.381 and 1.392 s under the profiler against
  0.414 and 1.547; two waves to a SIMD again.
- A weight prefetch two k-tiles ahead in that four-wave block: slower (0.423 and 1.492 s).
- One wave on 64 columns, which halves the activation traffic by itself: 4.75 and 4.51 ms in the
  microbenchmark, no better than the committed kernel.
- 32-row groups: 4.46 ms at 1,024 rows. Every group reads its expert's weights again, and 325
  groups read them 1.75 times as often as 185.
- Skipping padding fragments before the occupancy change: 3% (2.55 to 2.48 ms). The matrix time
  follows the waves in flight, not the products issued.

The microbenchmark slows by about 20% after a few back-to-back rounds and recovers after a pause,
so variants are timed interleaved in one process and compared within a regime.
