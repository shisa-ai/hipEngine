# the down projection is issue-bound, not traffic-bound: a single-kernel microbench kills the activation-re-read plan

## Summary

**The plan the previous entry named -- cut the down projection's activation
re-reads by giving each thread more output columns -- was aimed at the wrong
resource, and a single-kernel microbench says so before the kernel rewrite was
paid for.**

`scripts/gguf_q5_1_down_mmq_microbench.py` runs the production kernel at gemma4's
exact shape (128 experts, 4096 rows, Q5_1 out 2816, in 704, `planes=1`,
`f32_scales=True`) with the fixture's expert counts under control. Two results.

**1. The kernel fetches 5.96x its weight bytes, and the extra bytes cost nothing.**

| counts | row passes | FETCH_SIZE/call | ms/call | GB/s |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| uniform, 32 rows/expert | 1.00 | **1134.5 MB** | 3.150 | 360 |
| Poisson, mean 32 | 1.50 | 1360.3 MB | 3.134 | 434 |

The weights are 190.3 MB and the activations 3.9 MB, so one pass still fetches
1134.5 MB -- **5.96x the stored bytes**. Adding 50 percent more row passes adds
20 percent more bytes, and **changes the time by zero** (3.150 against 3.134 ms,
inside run-to-run noise). A kernel whose cost is invariant to a 20 percent change
in fetched bytes is not memory-bound at this size, whatever its byte count is.

That also retires the previous entry's reading of its own numbers. `ROWS_PER_PASS`
16 to 32 did cut 27.4 GB and did gain 2.5 percent, but the gain came from the
kernel being **at** the DRAM roofline at 16 rows (527 GB/s) and **off** it at 32
(333 GB/s); the bytes removed were on the critical path only because the kernel
was saturated. Now that it is not, more byte reduction buys nothing.

**2. Time scales with rows, not with passes.** Uniform 32 rows is 2.696 ms/call,
Poisson mean 32 (1.5x the passes) is 2.828 ms, and the isolated series is
1.640 ms at 16 rows, 2.696 at 32, 5.197 at 64 -- the 32-to-64 step doubles both
the rows and the passes and costs 1.93x, barely more than doubling the rows alone
would. The row-loop body is what the clock is spent on.

Per (thread, row, K32 sub-block) the body issues 16 `dp4a` -- eight for the Q5_1
nibble plane and eight for the high-bit plane, which is inherent to a 5-bit
weight -- against roughly the same number of loads, scale reads and FMAs. So
about half the row loop is the dot product and half is everything around it, and
the kernel runs at 3.0 T MAC/s. **The lever is the row loop's instruction count,
not its bytes**, and the two candidates are specializing the common
`rcount == ROWS_PER_PASS` case so the loop can unroll (its comment records that
it is not unrolled because `rcount` is dynamic) and hoisting the per-(row, K32)
scale reads out of the sub-block loop.

**3. A cross-kernel pattern worth one investigation.** Bytes fetched per stored
weight byte, at 512 tokens: the down projection 6.32x in production (6.0x in
isolation), the gate/up projection 2.51x, and the dense `gguf_q8_0_prefill_wmma`
69.6 MB per call against its largest weight matrix of 12.25 MB. Every one of
these is far above 1. The down projection proves that a 6x overfetch can be
free, so this is not by itself a defect -- but a *systematic* factor near 6
across three unrelated kernels is more likely to be one mechanism than three.
Recorded as a lead, not a finding: nothing here establishes the cause.

**4. The prefill has no single hot kernel that is off by 3x.** Every GEMM in the
step sits at 6.7-12 TFLOP/s, and the four largest kernels are at 13-333 GB/s
against a machine that reaches 527 GB/s when a kernel actually saturates it. The
1.33x to llama.cpp is spread evenly across the step, which is why the last three
iterations each moved the metric by 2-3 percent rather than finding a 30 percent
defect.

## Changes

- `scripts/gguf_q5_1_down_mmq_microbench.py` (new): the instrument. Real geometry
  by default, `--distribution {uniform,poisson}` for the expert counts, timings
  from device events around the kernel only (the activation pack and the uploads
  are outside the loop), and a JSON result including the row-pass count so the
  pass model can be checked against the clock. Run it under
  `rocprofv3 --pmc FETCH_SIZE` for the byte column.

No production kernel, route, dispatch or default changed.

## Validation

```
# isolated timings, gemma4's real shape
env -u HIP_VISIBLE_DEVICES ROCR_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0 HIPENGINE_HIP_ARCH=gfx1151 \
  PYTHONPATH=. .venv/bin/python scripts/gguf_q5_1_down_mmq_microbench.py \
  --rows-per-expert 32 --distribution uniform --warmup 5 --iters 25
# -> 2.696 ms/call median, 3.03 T MAC/s
# -> --distribution poisson: 2.828 ms/call, row_passes_ratio 1.5

# bytes
rocprofv3 --pmc FETCH_SIZE --output-format csv -d <dir> -- \
  .venv/bin/python scripts/gguf_q5_1_down_mmq_microbench.py \
  --rows-per-expert 32 --distribution uniform --warmup 1 --iters 3
# -> 1134.5 MB/call; poisson -> 1360.3 MB/call
```

The fixture is the production configuration, not an approximation of it: the
`planes=1` and `f32_scales=True` arguments and the 160-byte fp32-scale DS4 block
were read off the route in `gemma4_experts.py`, and the first version of the
harness got both wrong (3 planes, fp16 scales) and timed 6.49 ms/call instead of
2.67. The isolated 2.70 ms against production's 3.5 ms/call is attributed to L2
pressure from the 29 other layers interleaved in the real step, and is not
claimed as headroom.

## Next

1. **Specialize the row loop's common case.** `rcount == ROWS_PER_PASS` holds for
   every full pass, which is most of them; the dynamic bound is why the loop
   cannot unroll. A specialized full-pass path plus a hoisted scale read is the
   named lever, and the microbench is the instrument to price it against the
   2.696 ms/call baseline.
2. **Then re-check the gate/up projection**, which is the second largest kernel at
   153 ms/step and 135 GB/s. It is not bandwidth-bound either, so the same
   question -- what does its inner loop issue -- applies.
